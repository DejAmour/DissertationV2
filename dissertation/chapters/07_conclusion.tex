\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}

This dissertation examined when a neural control variate can improve the statistical and computational efficiency of Monte Carlo pricing for discretely monitored arithmetic Asian calls. Standard Monte Carlo, antithetic variates, a geometric Asian control variate and an analytically integrable neural control were compared under 12-date and 252-date monitoring profiles. The analysis separated out-of-sample variance reduction from setup and marginal cost, and then tested whether the cost of training a reference network could be shared across related contracts.

The principal statistical result is conditional on the monitoring profile and training policy. Under the 12-date profile, the contract-specific NCV trained for 1{,}000 epochs had lower variance than GCV for all seven contracts and in all thirty replications. Its variance advantage over GCV ranged from 11.18 to 23.31. Under the 252-date profile, NCV still reduced variance relative to MC for every contract and exceeded AV for five of the seven contracts, but GCV remained stronger throughout. Its variance advantage over GCV remained below one, between 0.009 and 0.281. A comparison only with MC would therefore have overstated the general performance of the neural method.

The checkpoint evidence explains part of this contrast. Longer training materially improved the 12-date network, while extending the 252-date network from 25 to 1{,}000 epochs did not improve its out-of-sample position relative to GCV. Training loss continued to decline for the larger network without a corresponding improvement in validation residual variance. This confirms that the relevant selection criterion is the performance of the frozen control on independent paths rather than its in-sample approximation loss. It also leaves open whether a larger training sample or a different regularisation and architecture policy could improve the 252-date result.

The computational analysis shows why a large variance reduction is not sufficient on its own. Under the single-valuation 50{,}000-path budget, AV was limited to 25{,}000 paired observations, GCV to 49{,}000 pricing observations after its pilot and scratch NCV to 45{,}000 after training. These deductions narrowed the equal-observation gains but did not change the central profile-specific ordering: NCV remained more precise than GCV for every 12-date contract, while GCV remained more precise for every 252-date contract. For the 12-date contracts, the lower marginal path requirement eventually recovered the cost of scratch training. The projected median-runtime break-even against GCV occurred after 609 to 703 repeated valuations, and the projected saving at 1{,}000 valuations ranged from 26 to 37 per cent. These figures show that NCV can become computationally attractive when the same trained control is used repeatedly. They are workload and hardware dependent, however, and should not be interpreted as universal production thresholds. Under the 252-date profile, the higher matched-accuracy marginal cost and positive training cost prevented a corresponding break-even.

The frozen-reuse experiment produced a more cautious result. Applying the reference network with a fixed coefficient was not competitive, and estimating a target-specific coefficient improved performance without making reuse generally superior to GCV. Only the 12-date long-maturity transfer exceeded GCV, with a variance advantage of 1.83 and a projected break-even after 1{,}490 valuations. Reusing one network across the complete six-contract cycle produced no finite break-even. The training cost can therefore be amortised only if the learned control retains sufficient information about each target payoff. Cost sharing cannot compensate for weak transfer performance.

The study contributes a controlled comparison in which the neural method is assessed against an unusually strong problem-specific control rather than only against crude Monte Carlo. It also makes the economic role of training explicit by separating one-time setup from pricing-only marginal work and by reporting matched-accuracy break-even workloads. The crossed training-horizon experiment and the frozen-reuse stage further show that neither additional epochs nor shared setup should be assumed to improve practical efficiency without independent statistical evidence.

The conclusions remain bounded by the use of GBM, one network architecture, fixed training and pilot sizes, a limited contract grid and CPU-specific timing projections. The 12-date and 252-date profiles are complete experimental regimes rather than a causal experiment on monitoring dimension alone. Further work should first test whether more data and regularisation close the 252-date generalisation gap. It should then examine parameter-conditioned or lightly fine-tuned controls and pricing models in which the geometric benchmark is less dominant, including models with mean reversion or stochastic volatility.

The research question can therefore be answered directly. A trained NCV can improve arithmetic Asian option pricing when it achieves a validated out-of-sample reduction beyond the available analytical control and is reused enough times to recover training cost. Those conditions were satisfied by the contract-specific 12-date networks, but not by the 252-date networks or by frozen reuse across most of the tested contracts. The practical value of the method lies not in replacing classical variance reduction in every setting, but in identifying pricing problems for which learned structure remains both statistically effective and economically reusable.
