\chapter{Methodology}
\label{chap:methodology}

This chapter defines the pricing problem and the four Monte Carlo estimators compared in the empirical analysis. Standard Monte Carlo provides the baseline. Antithetic variates modify the sampling scheme. The geometric and neural control variates each subtract a centred auxiliary quantity whose expectation is available analytically. The chapter closes by defining the efficiency measures used to compare them. Contract specifications, training hyperparameters and path allocations are reserved for Chapter~4.

\section{Risk-neutral pricing framework}

Let $S_t$ denote the underlying asset price at time $t$. Under the risk-neutral measure, the asset follows geometric Brownian motion with constant risk-free rate $r$ and volatility $\sigma$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{d}S_t = rS_t\,\mathrm{d}t + \sigma S_t\,\mathrm{d}W_t,
\label{eq:gbm_sde}
\end{equation}
where $W_t$ is a standard Brownian motion. The solution over a monitoring interval $\Delta t$ can be simulated exactly. For equally spaced monitoring dates $t_j = jT/m$, $j=1,\dots,m$, the recursive path equation is
\begin{equation}
S_j = S_{j-1}\exp\left[\left(r-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_j\right],
\label{eq:gbm_discrete}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta t = T/m$ and $Z_1,\dots,Z_m$ are independent standard normal draws. A simulated path is therefore a deterministic function of the Gaussian vector $\mathbf{Z}=(Z_1,\dots,Z_m)$. This representation is shared by all four estimators and is essential for the neural control, whose analytical expectation relies on the inputs being Gaussian.

Two monitoring profiles are examined: $m=12$ (monthly) and $m=252$ (approximately business-daily) over a one-year maturity. They are treated as distinct pricing regimes. Increasing $m$ changes both the discretely monitored payoff and the dimension of the neural-network input, so the comparison is not interpreted as an isolated experiment on monitoring frequency.

\subsection{Arithmetic Asian payoff}

The arithmetic average and discounted call payoff on a simulated path are
\begin{align}
A(\mathbf{Z}) &= \frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m} S_j,
\label{eq:arithmetic_average}\\
f(\mathbf{Z}) &= e^{-rT}\max\bigl(A(\mathbf{Z})-K,\,0\bigr),
\label{eq:arithmetic_payoff}
\end{align}
where $K$ is the strike and $T$ is the maturity. The no-arbitrage price is $V=\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{Z})]$. Because the arithmetic average is a sum of correlated lognormal variables, this expectation has no convenient closed form. Simulation is therefore necessary, and the methodological problem is to estimate $V$ with less sampling variation or lower total cost.

\section{Standard Monte Carlo}

For independent draws $\mathbf{Z}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{Z}^{(n)}$, the standard Monte Carlo estimator is the sample mean of the discounted payoffs:
\begin{equation}
\hat{V}_{\mathrm{MC}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} f\bigl(\mathbf{Z}^{(i)}\bigr).
\label{eq:mc_estimator}
\end{equation}

This estimator is unbiased with variance $\mathrm{Var}[f(\mathbf{Z})]/n$. By the central limit theorem, an approximate $100(1-\alpha)\%$ confidence interval is
\begin{equation}
\hat{V}_{\mathrm{MC}} \pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-1}\frac{s_f}{\sqrt{n}},
\label{eq:mc_ci}
\end{equation}
where $s_f$ is the sample standard deviation of the payoffs. Standard error falls at rate $O(n^{-1/2})$: halving it requires roughly four times as many paths. This makes standard Monte Carlo a useful but expensive baseline \parencite{boyle_1977,glasserman_2004}. Its variance, runtime and confidence-interval width serve as the reference values throughout the empirical chapters.

\section{Antithetic variates}

Antithetic variates exploit the symmetry of the standard normal distribution. If $\mathbf{Z}$ has the required distribution, so does $-\mathbf{Z}$. Each draw is paired with its negative and the two payoffs are averaged:
\begin{equation}
Y_{\mathrm{AV}}(\mathbf{Z}) = \frac{1}{2}\left[f(\mathbf{Z}) + f(-\mathbf{Z})\right].
\label{eq:av_observation}
\end{equation}

The estimator averages $n$ independent pair values. It is unbiased because both elements of each pair have expectation $V$. The variance of a single pair is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Var}(Y_{\mathrm{AV}})=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathrm{Var}(f)\left[1+\mathrm{Corr}\bigl(f(\mathbf{Z}),f(-\mathbf{Z})\bigr)\right].
\label{eq:av_variance}
\end{equation}

Negative within-pair correlation reduces variance. For a monotone payoff driven by Gaussian shocks this typically holds, though the magnitude depends on the specific payoff. The method requires no training or pilot estimation, making it a low-setup benchmark \parencite{hammersley_morton_1956}.

Path accounting requires care. One estimator observation consumes two payoff evaluations. An equal-observation comparison treats each pair as one observation. An equal-path comparison gives the method the same total number of simulated paths as its competitors. Both conventions are reported in the empirical chapters.

\section{Geometric Asian control variate}

A control variate is a random quantity computed on the same path as the target payoff whose expectation is known. For arithmetic Asian pricing, the natural choice is the geometric Asian payoff. Define
\begin{align}
G(\mathbf{Z}) &= \left(\prod_{j=1}^{m} S_j\right)^{1/m},
\label{eq:geometric_average}\\
g(\mathbf{Z}) &= e^{-rT}\max\bigl(G(\mathbf{Z})-K,\,0\bigr).
\label{eq:geometric_payoff}
\end{align}

Under GBM with equally spaced dates, $\log G$ is normally distributed with mean and variance
\begin{align}
\mu_G &= \log S_0 + \left(r-\tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)T\frac{m+1}{2m},
\label{eq:mu_g}\\
\nu_G^2 &= \sigma^2T\frac{(m+1)(2m+1)}{6m^2}.
\label{eq:nu_g}
\end{align}

The analytical price of the geometric Asian call follows from the lognormal call formula:
\begin{align}
\theta_G &= e^{-rT}\left[\exp\left(\mu_G+\tfrac{1}{2}\nu_G^2\right)\Phi(d_1)-K\Phi(d_2)\right],
\label{eq:theta_g}\\
d_1 &= \frac{\mu_G-\log K+\nu_G^2}{\nu_G},
\label{eq:d1_g}\\
d_2 &= d_1-\nu_G.
\label{eq:d2_g}
\end{align}

The control-variate observation is
\begin{equation}
Y_{\mathrm{GCV}} = f(\mathbf{Z}) - \beta\left[g(\mathbf{Z})-\theta_G\right].
\label{eq:gcv_observation}
\end{equation}

For any fixed $\beta$, the centred control has expectation zero and the estimator is unbiased. The variance-minimising coefficient is $\beta^*=\mathrm{Cov}(f,g)/\mathrm{Var}(g)$, which yields a residual variance of $\mathrm{Var}(f)\left[1-\rho(f,g)^2\right]$. Since the arithmetic and geometric averages are computed from the same prices, they are typically strongly correlated, making this control unusually effective \parencite{kemna_vorst_1990}.

In practice, $\beta^*$ is unknown and is estimated by ordinary least squares on an independent pilot sample. The estimate is frozen before final pricing begins. Because the pricing paths are independent of the pilot and the control is centred using its analytical expectation, the final estimator remains unbiased conditional on the pilot. In computational comparisons, pilot simulation is treated as a setup cost; when a fixed path budget is imposed, pilot paths are deducted from the paths available for pricing.

\section{Neural control variate}

The neural control variate replaces the geometric payoff with a learned function of the Gaussian input. A general neural network might approximate $f$ well, but it is not automatically a valid control: its expectation must also be known. Estimating that expectation by a second simulation would transfer rather than solve the integration problem. The architecture used here follows the analytically integrable construction of \textcite{el_filali_ech-chafiq_lelong_reghai_2021}. It has one hidden layer of rectified linear units (ReLUs) and a linear output:
\begin{equation}
H_{\theta}(\mathbf{Z}) = b_2 + \sum_{k=1}^{h} w_{2k}\,\max\!\bigl(\mathbf{w}_{1k}^\top \mathbf{Z} + b_{1k},\,0\bigr),
\label{eq:shallow_network}
\end{equation}
where $h$ is the number of hidden units and $\theta$ collects all weights and biases. The shallow structure is a deliberate constraint: it is flexible enough to learn nonlinear functions of the path shocks while retaining an analytically computable expectation. Deeper architectures would generally destroy this tractability.

\subsection{Analytical network expectation}

For hidden unit $k$, define the pre-activation $U_k = \mathbf{w}_{1k}^\top \mathbf{Z} + b_{1k}$. Since $\mathbf{Z}$ is standard normal, $U_k$ is univariate normal with mean $\mu_k=b_{1k}$ and variance $s_k^2 = \mathbf{w}_{1k}^\top \mathbf{w}_{1k}$. For a normal random variable $X$ with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $s$, the expected positive part is
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[\max(X,0)] = s\,\phi(\mu/s) + \mu\,\Phi(\mu/s),
\label{eq:relu_moment}
\end{equation}
where $\phi$ and $\Phi$ are the standard normal density and distribution function. Applying this to each hidden unit and using linearity of expectation gives
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[H_{\theta}(\mathbf{Z})] = b_2 + \sum_{k=1}^{h} w_{2k}\left[s_k\,\phi(\mu_k/s_k)+\mu_k\,\Phi(\mu_k/s_k)\right].
\label{eq:network_expectation}
\end{equation}

The pre-activations of different hidden units may be correlated, but this does not matter: the output expectation is the weighted sum of individual unit expectations. Units with $s_k\approx 0$ are evaluated at the deterministic limit $\max(\mu_k,0)$. The full derivation of \eqref{eq:relu_moment} is in the appendix.

\subsection{Training and final estimator}

The network is fitted on a training sample of payoff--input pairs. The objective is to minimise the empirical variance of $f(\mathbf{Z})-H_{\theta}(\mathbf{Z})$, not to predict a label in the usual supervised-learning sense. A separate validation sample monitors generalisation. Once training is complete, $\theta$ is frozen and no pricing-sample observations influence the network. This separation matters because a flexible control can overfit training noise, producing low in-sample loss but poor out-of-sample variance reduction \parencite{wan_zhong_xiong_zhu_2020}.

With frozen parameters, the neural control-variate observation is
\begin{equation}
Y_{\mathrm{NCV}} = f(\mathbf{Z}) - \beta\left[H_{\theta}(\mathbf{Z})-\mathbb{E}[H_{\theta}(\mathbf{Z})]\right].
\label{eq:ncv_observation}
\end{equation}

The pricing estimator averages independent copies of \eqref{eq:ncv_observation}. It is unbiased for any $\beta$ fixed independently of the pricing sample. In contract-specific NCV, both $\theta$ and $\beta$ are fitted to the target contract: $\theta$ by training, $\beta$ by OLS on a disjoint pilot sample (the same logic as for GCV). Setting $\beta=1$ tests the network as trained, without target-sample rescaling.

Cross-contract reuse freezes the network $\theta_0$ trained on a reference contract and applies it to a different target contract. Two variants are considered:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Frozen $\beta=1$:} the reference network is applied directly with no target-specific estimation, incurring no pilot cost.
    \item \textbf{Frozen estimated $\beta$:} a small independent target pilot estimates the optimal linear coefficient between the target payoff and $H_{\theta_0}$. This can correct for changes in scale when the learned output remains informative, but cannot recover structure the network never learned.
\end{itemize}
Reuse is examined within each monitoring profile because the $m=12$ and $m=252$ networks have different input dimensions.

\section{Efficiency measures}

The empirical comparison separates statistical performance from computational cost.

\subsection{Statistical measures}

The primary measure is the variance-reduction factor:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{VRF}_M = \frac{\mathrm{Var}(\hat{V}_{\mathrm{MC}})}{\mathrm{Var}(\hat{V}_M)}.
\label{eq:vrf}
\end{equation}
A value above one indicates lower sampling variance than MC under the stated observation convention. Complementary measures include bias relative to a reference price, root mean squared error, standard error and confidence-interval width. Variance reduction alone is not treated as sufficient evidence, because an estimator can have low variance but introduce finite-sample bias from implementation or numerical error.

\subsection{Computational measures}

Computational accounting separates setup cost $S_M$ from marginal pricing cost $M_M$. MC and AV have zero model-training setup. GCV setup consists of the pilot. Scratch NCV includes training-data generation and optimiser time. Frozen reuse includes the shared reference training and, for estimated-$\beta$ reuse, a target-specific pilot. For $Q$ valuations, total cost is
\begin{equation}
C_M(Q)=S_M + Q\,M_M.
\label{eq:total_cost}
\end{equation}
When method A has higher setup but lower marginal cost than method B, the break-even point is
\begin{equation}
Q^* = \frac{S_A-S_B}{M_B-M_A}.
\label{eq:break_even}
\end{equation}
The reported value is the smallest integer $Q$ at which A is no more expensive. If A has both higher setup and no lower marginal cost, no finite break-even exists.

\subsection{Comparison conventions}

Three conventions are reported to prevent a variance gain from being mistaken for a computational gain:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Equal-observation:} fixes the number of estimator observations, isolating variance reduction.
    \item \textbf{Equal-budget:} imposes a common simulated-path allowance, deducting pilot paths.
    \item \textbf{Matched-accuracy:} estimates the path count and runtime each method needs to attain the baseline's precision.
\end{enumerate}
For antithetic variates, both pair counts and total path counts are identified.

\section{Summary}

All four estimators target the same risk-neutral expectation. MC averages the discounted payoff directly. AV modifies the sampling scheme. GCV subtracts a centred geometric payoff whose price is known analytically. NCV subtracts a learned shallow network whose expectation is computed from normal positive-part moments. Independent training, pilot, validation and pricing samples preserve the interpretation of the final estimates. Chapter~4 converts these definitions into the two-stage experimental design.
