\chapter{Discussion}
\label{chap:discussion}

The results answer the research question conditionally. A contract-specific neural control variate can improve arithmetic Asian option pricing, but its value depends on whether the trained approximation generalises to independent paths, whether a strong analytical control is already available and whether the network is used often enough to recover its setup cost. The contrast between the 12-date and 252-date profiles is therefore more informative than a single overall ranking. It shows both the potential of the analytical neural construction and the conditions under which that potential is not realised.

\section{Interpreting the contract-specific results}

The performance of GCV provides the natural starting point. It reduced variance by roughly two hundred to more than four thousand times across both profiles and exceeded AV for every contract. This is consistent with the role of the geometric average in arithmetic Asian option pricing described by \textcite{kemna_vorst_1990}. The geometric and arithmetic payoffs are generated from the same path and retain a close relationship under GBM, while the expectation of the geometric payoff is known. The benchmark is therefore unusually difficult to beat. A neural method can improve substantially on MC and still add little when judged against GCV.

Against this benchmark, the 12-date result is strong. At the selected 1{,}000-epoch checkpoint, contract-specific NCV had lower variance than GCV for every contract and in every replication. The result supports the analytical construction used by \textcite{el_filali_ech-chafiq_lelong_reghai_2021}: a shallow ReLU network can learn enough of the pricing integrand to act as an effective control while retaining a closed-form expectation. It also shows that analytical tractability does not necessarily prevent the network from improving on a highly specialised financial control. This conclusion is limited to the trained 12-date regime, however, rather than to NCV as a general method.

The 252-date evidence gives the necessary qualification. NCV reduced variance relative to MC for all seven contracts and exceeded AV for five of them, so the network was not ineffective. It nevertheless remained behind GCV throughout. The distinction matters because a comparison only with MC would have produced a favourable but incomplete assessment. In this setting, the relevant question is not whether the learned control removes variation, but whether it removes more residual variation than the analytical alternative after allowing for training and evaluation cost.

The two profiles should not be interpreted as a controlled estimate of the effect of monitoring frequency. Moving from 12 to 252 dates changes the discretised payoff, input dimension, parameter count and selected checkpoint. The $2 \times 2$ sensitivity experiment does, however, rule out one simple explanation. Extending the 252-date network from 25 to 1{,}000 epochs did not improve its position relative to GCV, while longer training materially improved the 12-date network. The reversal is therefore not explained merely by having stopped the 252-date optimisation too early.

\section{Training, dimension and out-of-sample performance}

The checkpoint curves show why training loss alone is not an adequate selection criterion. For the 252-date network, training loss continued to decline while validation residual variance did not improve consistently. This is the behaviour that out-of-sample evaluation is intended to detect. It is also consistent with the warning in \textcite{wan_zhong_xiong_zhu_2020} that a flexible learned control may fit its training sample without producing an equally strong reduction on new Monte Carlo observations. The appropriate outcome is the variance of the frozen estimator on independent paths, not the final in-sample loss.

A plausible contributor is the fixed training sample across architectures. The 12-date network contains 449 trainable parameters, whereas the 252-date network contains 8{,}129, but both use 5{,}000 training paths. The larger model therefore has substantially more capacity relative to the available sample. This asymmetry does not prove that sample size caused the weaker validation result, since optimisation and the complexity of the payoff also change. It does mean that 5{,}000 paths should be regarded as sufficient for the reported experiment rather than as generally sufficient for a 252-input neural control.

The selected checkpoints require a similarly narrow interpretation. For $m = 12$, validation performance was still improving at 1{,}000 epochs, so this value is the best point on the evaluated grid rather than a demonstrated global optimum. For $m = 252$, 25 epochs was selected because later checkpoints did not improve the held-out residual, not because high-dimensional networks ordinarily converge in fewer epochs. These choices are defensible because they follow independent validation evidence, but they do not replace a broader study of training size, regularisation, width and learning rate.

\section{From variance reduction to computational value}

The matched-accuracy analysis changes the interpretation of the 12-date variance result. Scratch NCV is not the cheapest method for an isolated price because its network must first be trained. Its lower residual variance reduces the number of pricing paths required after training, producing a projected break-even against GCV after approximately 600 to 700 repeated valuations. At 1{,}000 valuations, the projected saving of 26 to 37 per cent is material. The result illustrates why setup and marginal cost should be reported separately rather than combined into one runtime observation.

The break-even count is not a universal measure of desk efficiency. It is a projection from measured CPU timings and replication-level variance estimates, and several timing series required piecewise rather than linear extrapolation. It also assumes repeated use for the same pricing problem. A trading or risk system may value a contract many times, but market updates and scenario changes can alter the payoff mapping for which the network was trained. The 600 to 700 range is therefore evidence that amortisation is possible under repeated comparable valuations, not proof that the cost would be recovered after a fixed number of calendar updates on every desk.

No corresponding economic case emerged for the 252-date profile. NCV required approximately 3.6 to 111 times as many observations as GCV to match sampling variance. The measured pricing-time difference was too small to offset this statistical disadvantage, and NCV retained a positive setup cost. Increasing the number of valuations therefore does not rescue a method with the higher matched-accuracy marginal cost. This is an example of the broader point made by \textcite{portier_segers_2019}: a more flexible control space may improve on crude Monte Carlo without improving total computational efficiency.

\section{Interpretation of frozen reuse}

Frozen reuse was intended to create a more favourable cost structure by sharing one training expense across related contracts. The beta-one results show that reuse is not automatic. A network trained on the reference contract did not remain correctly scaled for the target payoffs, and its variance advantage over GCV stayed below 0.016 throughout. Estimating a target-specific coefficient improved performance because it could correct a remaining linear change in scale. It could not reconstruct payoff information that was absent from the frozen representation.

The strike results are particularly intuitive. Changing the strike moves the region in which the option finishes in the money and changes the location of the payoff kink. A network trained around the reference strike may therefore be well aligned with the reference payoff but poorly aligned with observations that become important at a distant strike. Changing volatility alters the mapping from the same Gaussian shocks to the asset path and produces a related distribution shift. In both cases, GCV preserved a strong direct relationship with the target arithmetic payoff, while the frozen network generally did not.

Maturity changes transferred more successfully, especially for the 12-date long-maturity contract. One possible interpretation is that the network retained a useful direction associated with the average path and that coefficient re-estimation corrected much of the change in scale. This explanation is plausible rather than identified by the experiment. Only the long-maturity transfer exceeded GCV, and its projected break-even was later than for a contract-specific network. The full six-contract cycle had no finite break-even, so sharing training cost was not enough to make the frozen method competitive across the tested grid.

The reuse experiment is narrower than transfer learning in the general sense of \textcite{pan_yang_2010}. The weights were frozen, the architecture did not receive contract parameters as inputs and no fine-tuning was allowed. The result should therefore be read as evidence about direct reuse of this reference network, not as evidence that learned information cannot be shared across an option surface. It identifies the weakness of the simplest reuse policy and motivates designs in which the contract parameters are part of the learned mapping.

\section{Limitations and directions for further work}

The first limitation is the pricing model. GBM provides a controlled environment and preserves the analytical geometric benchmark, but it omits features that are important for commodities and other underlying assets, including mean reversion, seasonality, stochastic volatility and jumps. The present results therefore establish estimator behaviour under GBM rather than pricing performance under a realistic commodity model. The strength of GCV in this setting should also be kept in perspective. Neural controls may be relatively more useful when no comparable analytical payoff is available, although that claim must be tested rather than assumed.

The experimental grid is deliberately bounded. It contains one reference parameter set, six perturbations, one hidden width, one training size and one pilot size. The two monitoring profiles are co-primary regimes, but they do not isolate the effect of dimension. Reference values are themselves high-precision simulations, so very small bias differences remain uncertain. Finally, runtime results depend on the recorded CPU environment and are projected rather than observed over hundreds of complete production valuations. These limitations affect the breadth and numerical portability of the conclusions, but not the reported ordering within the tested samples.

The immediate next step is to determine whether the 252-date generalisation gap can be reduced. A targeted experiment should vary training sample size and regularisation while retaining an independent validation set, rather than extending epochs alone. Width could then be scaled with the input dimension instead of fixed across profiles. Pilot-size sensitivity would show whether coefficient noise contributes materially to the final residual variance. These tests would distinguish an architectural limitation from a data limitation more directly than another isolated long training run.

A second direction is to train across a distribution of contract parameters. Strike, volatility and maturity could enter the network alongside the simulation shocks, allowing one model to learn a local option surface rather than one reference payoff. A smaller fine-tuning experiment would provide an intermediate comparison between fully frozen reuse and scratch training. A hybrid estimator could also train a neural control on the residual left after GCV, or after another approximate financial control. This would use known structure first and ask the network to learn only what the classical control fails to remove.

The most important extension is to pricing models for which the classical benchmark is weaker. Mean-reverting or stochastic-volatility models driven by Gaussian innovations preserve a natural input for the analytical shallow network, while changing the relationship between arithmetic and geometric payoffs. The statistical comparison should then be repeated together with hardware-specific timing, including batched or accelerated network evaluation. Such evidence would show whether the present negative 252-date and reuse results are features of the neural construction or consequences of testing it where analytical information is already exceptionally strong.

Overall, NCV is best viewed as a conditional tool rather than a replacement for classical variance reduction. It was highly effective for the well-trained 12-date contracts and potentially economical under repeated use. It was not competitive with GCV for the 252-date contracts, and direct frozen reuse rarely preserved enough information to justify its cost. The central practical requirement is therefore not simply a trainable network, but a validated reduction in out-of-sample residual variance that remains strong enough, for long enough, to recover the work used to obtain it.
