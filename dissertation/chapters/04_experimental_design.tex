\chapter{Experimental Design}
\label{chap:experimental_design}

This chapter translates the estimators defined in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology} into a reproducible empirical design. The study has two stages. Stage~I is the main analysis and compares contract-specific neural control variates with standard Monte Carlo, antithetic variates and the geometric Asian control variate. Stage~II is an exploratory extension in which the reference network is frozen and reused for related contracts. Both stages are conducted separately for 12-date and 252-date monitoring profiles. Statistical precision and computational cost are recorded separately so that a reduction in variance is not automatically interpreted as a reduction in total pricing work.

\section{Contract specification and monitoring profiles}

The reference contract has initial asset price $S_0 = 100$, strike $K = 100$, risk-free rate $r = 0.05$, volatility $\sigma = 0.20$ and maturity $T = 1$ year. Six target contracts vary one parameter at a time while retaining the remaining reference values. This produces a compact grid that separates changes in moneyness, volatility and maturity. Every contract is priced under the same risk-neutral geometric Brownian motion model used in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}.

For each contract, the monitoring dates are equally spaced over its maturity. The 12-date profile represents a lower-dimensional discretely monitored contract, while the 252-date profile represents a higher-dimensional contract. They are co-primary regimes. Differences between them cannot be attributed to monitoring frequency alone because the discretised payoff, neural input dimension, parameter count and selected training horizon also change. Networks are therefore trained and reused within a profile, never transferred from 12 to 252 inputs or conversely.

\section{Sample separation and replication}

Training, validation, pilot and final pricing observations have distinct roles. Training paths fit the neural weights. Validation paths compare checkpoints in the separate training-curve experiment. Pilot paths estimate control coefficients. Final pricing paths measure out-of-sample estimator performance. No final pricing observation is used to train the network, select a checkpoint or estimate a control coefficient. This separation preserves conditional unbiasedness and reduces the risk of reporting in-sample variance reduction.

The principal comparison uses 5{,}000 training paths, a 1{,}000-path coefficient pilot and 50{,}000 final pricing paths in each replication. The pilot size is fixed as a practical balance between coefficient stability and the paths retained for pricing; it is not claimed to be optimal. A fresh independent pilot is generated in every replication, so variation caused by coefficient estimation is included in the reported results. Pilot-size sensitivity is therefore retained as a limitation. Thirty replications provide a distribution of price estimates, estimator variances and runtimes rather than a result from one favourable random seed. A base seed of 42 generates reproducible but distinct streams across replications and sample roles, and the realised seeds are retained with each run.

\section{Stage I: contract-specific comparison}

Stage~I trains a separate NCV for each of the seven contracts within each monitoring profile. MC, AV, GCV and NCV are then evaluated on independent final samples. The same contract parameters and replication structure are used across methods. This stage answers the main empirical question: whether a neural control trained for the target contract improves statistical and computational efficiency relative to both a method with almost no setup cost and a strong problem-specific analytical control.

Both profiles use the shallow architecture defined in Section~\ref{eq:shallow_network}: one hidden ReLU layer of width 32 followed by a linear output. With $m$ inputs, the architecture contains $32m + 65$ trainable parameters, giving 449 parameters for the 12-date network and 8{,}129 for the 252-date network. Training uses mean squared error, the Adam optimiser, a learning rate of 0.01, a batch size of 256, Xavier-uniform weight initialisation and zero initial biases. The experiments are executed on CPU under the recorded software environment.

The same 5{,}000-path training sample is retained across profiles to impose a common training-information budget, despite the large difference in parameter count. Validation and held-out test behaviour were consistent at the selected checkpoints, so 5{,}000 paths were treated as sufficient for the reported comparison, although no claim of sample-size optimality is made. For each profile, one network is trained continuously in each replication and recorded at epochs 0, 10, 25, 50, 100, 200, 500 and 1{,}000 without reinitialisation. The experiment uses 10{,}000 validation and 50{,}000 held-out test paths over ten replications. The reporting rule selects the checkpoint with the lowest mean validation residual variance, preferring the earliest checkpoint within 1\% when the minimum is effectively flat. Held-out test and final pricing outcomes do not enter this criterion.

The validation evidence selects 1{,}000 epochs for the 12-date profile and 25 epochs for the 252-date profile. For the 12-date network, 1{,}000 is the best checkpoint within the evaluated grid rather than a claim of global optimality, because validation performance was still improving at the upper boundary. For the 252-date network, further reductions in training loss did not produce a consistent improvement in validation residual variance. The selected horizon is fixed across all contracts within its profile. A separate $2 \times 2$ sensitivity design crosses $m \in \{12, 252\}$ with 25 and 1{,}000 epochs. This checks whether the qualitative comparison depends on training duration; it is not used to select a new checkpoint or to identify the causal effect of input dimension.

The statistical and timing calculations in the checkpoint study are kept separate. Checkpoint performance is evaluated on fixed independent samples, whereas runtime is measured using the pricing-path counts and repeats described above. This prevents timing noise from changing the statistical sample and allows additional training cost to be compared with the out-of-sample reduction in residual variance.

\section{Stage II: exploratory frozen reuse}

Stage~II is deliberately narrower than Stage~I. The network trained on the reference contract is frozen and applied to the six target contracts within the same monitoring profile. No fine-tuning is performed. The exercise is exploratory because its purpose is to test whether shared payoff structure survives moderate parameter changes, not to claim a general transfer-learning method.

Two frozen estimators are considered. Frozen beta-one reuse applies the reference network with $\beta = 1$ and requires no target-specific pilot. Frozen estimated-beta reuse uses a new 1{,}000-path target pilot to rescale the frozen output before pricing. The first isolates the value of the original learned approximation. The second tests whether a cheap linear adjustment can recover performance when the network remains informative but its scale changes. Reference-network training is a shared setup cost and is counted once across a sequence of target valuations.

\section{Comparison conventions}

Three comparison conventions answer different questions. The equal-observation comparison holds the number of final estimator observations fixed and measures statistical variance reduction. For AV, one observation is the mean of a path generated from $\mathbf{Z}$ and a second path generated from $-\mathbf{Z}$. The number of pair observations and the number of simulated paths are therefore reported separately.

The path-budget comparison uses a 50{,}000-path target-valuation allowance under method-specific conventions. MC allocates all 50{,}000 paths to pricing, while AV forms at most 25{,}000 pair observations. GCV uses 1{,}000 paths to estimate $\beta$ and the remaining 49{,}000 for pricing. For NCV, the 5{,}000 training paths are treated as setup and are not deducted from the 50{,}000 final pricing paths; where $\beta$ is estimated, its 1{,}000-path pilot is also a setup sample. NCV therefore receives more total simulated paths than MC under this comparison. The comparison is retained to isolate pricing-sample performance, but it is not described as equal total work and is interpreted alongside setup-inclusive timing and matched-accuracy results.

The matched-accuracy comparison uses replication-level variance estimates to determine the path count required to reach a common target variance or RMSE. This estimated requirement is combined with measured marginal runtime. Runtime is benchmarked at 1{,}000, 5{,}000 and 10{,}000 pricing paths with three repeats; when the linearity diagnostic fails, a piecewise empirical projection is used rather than an unsupported linear extrapolation. The calculation is interpreted cautiously when estimated bias is material. Price, bias, RMSE, estimator variance, standard error and confidence-interval width are retained alongside variance-reduction ratios.

\section{Computational accounting and break-even analysis}

Runtime is separated into setup and marginal components. MC and AV have zero estimator setup. GCV setup is its target pilot. Scratch NCV setup comprises training-path generation and optimiser time and, where an estimated coefficient is used, the target pilot. Frozen beta-one reuse has the shared reference training cost but no target pilot. Frozen estimated-beta reuse adds the target pilot to that shared cost. Marginal time contains only the work required to generate pricing paths, evaluate the payoff and evaluate the control used by that estimator. Validation generation and checkpoint evaluation are treated as research overhead rather than operational setup because they are used to establish the fixed training policy, not repeated for each valuation.

\begin{equation}
C(M,Q) = S(M) + Q\,M(M),
\label{eq:experimental_total_cost}
\end{equation}
where $S(M)$ is measured setup cost, $M(M)$ is marginal cost per valuation and $Q$ is the number of valuations. Break-even is the smallest integer $Q \geq 1$ for which the higher-setup method is no more expensive than the comparator at matched accuracy. The $Q = 1$ boundary is checked directly. If the candidate method has no lower marginal cost, no finite break-even is reported. The calculation also identifies the workload immediately below break-even where such a workload exists.

\section{Validation and reproducibility}

Validation is performed at three levels. First, the GBM path generator is checked against analytical moments and a European Black--Scholes benchmark. Second, the simulated geometric Asian payoff is compared with the closed-form value used to centre GCV. Third, the analytical expectation of the trained network is checked against an independent large-sample numerical average. These tests target the main sources of estimator error before variance comparisons are interpreted.

A high-precision independent reference price is used to calculate bias and RMSE for the arithmetic Asian payoff. Automated tests cover sample separation, confidence intervals, control-coefficient estimation, AV path accounting, setup-cost propagation and the $Q = 1$ break-even boundary. Run outputs retain contract parameters, monitoring profile, seeds, sample sizes, checkpoint, software environment and timing components. This record separates a reproducible design decision from a provisional empirical claim.

The Brent crude oil calculation is retained only as a model diagnostic. Historical return behaviour is used to illustrate limitations of constant volatility and Gaussian increments. It does not calibrate the option-pricing experiments and is not used to claim that the GBM prices are market prices for traded Brent contracts.

\section{Experimental summary}

The design gives priority to the contract-specific comparison while retaining a bounded test of reuse. Seven contracts are studied under two distinct monitoring profiles, with disjoint training, validation, pilot and pricing samples. Profile-specific checkpoints are selected from independent validation evidence and then fixed across contracts. Equal-observation, path-budget and matched-accuracy comparisons separate statistical gains from path and runtime costs, while the $2 \times 2$ sensitivity design tests whether the main conclusion changes under short and long training horizons. Stage~II then asks whether the reference training cost can be shared across related contracts without fine-tuning. Chapter~5 reports the resulting estimator performance and break-even workloads.
