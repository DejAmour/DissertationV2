\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

An Asian option is a path-dependent derivative whose payoff depends on an average of the underlying asset price over a set of monitoring dates. In the arithmetic case the monitored prices are summed and divided by the number of fixings before the strike is applied. Because the payoff depends on the entire price path, not just the terminal value, pricing requires information about the joint distribution of the asset price at every monitoring date.

Under geometric Brownian motion (GBM), the geometric average of lognormal prices is itself lognormal, so the corresponding geometric Asian option has a closed-form price. The arithmetic average is a sum of correlated lognormals and does not admit a comparable formula. Monte Carlo simulation is therefore the standard pricing method: simulate paths, evaluate the discounted payoff on each path and average the results \parencite{kemna_vorst_1990,glasserman_2004}.

Monte Carlo is flexible but converges slowly. Standard error falls only as $O(n^{-1/2})$, so halving the error requires four times as many paths. When prices must be updated across contracts, scenarios or reporting dates, this cost accumulates. Variance-reduction methods aim to obtain the same expectation with less sampling noise. This dissertation compares two classical methods---antithetic variates and a geometric Asian control variate---with a learned neural control variate, and asks under what conditions the learned method justifies its additional cost.

\section{The four estimators}

Standard Monte Carlo (MC) averages independent discounted payoffs. It serves as the baseline.

Antithetic variates (AV) pair each vector of Gaussian shocks $\mathbf{Z}$ with $-\mathbf{Z}$. If the two resulting payoffs are negatively correlated, their average has lower variance than either one alone. The method requires no analytical auxiliary and no training, but each estimator observation consumes two simulated paths.

Geometric control variate (GCV) exploits the fact that the geometric Asian payoff is computed from the same simulated path as the arithmetic payoff, is strongly correlated with it and has a known expectation. By subtracting a centred version of the geometric payoff, GCV can remove a large fraction of the arithmetic payoff's variance at negligible additional cost. This makes GCV an unusually strong classical benchmark.

Neural control variate (NCV) replaces the geometric payoff with the output of a shallow neural network. The network has one hidden layer of rectified linear units (ReLUs) and a linear output. Because each ReLU pre-activation is a linear combination of Gaussian inputs, its expectation can be evaluated analytically. The trained network can therefore be centred exactly, without a second Monte Carlo estimate of its mean, and used as a conventional control variate. The price of this flexibility is an upfront training cost and a per-path evaluation cost that do not arise with GCV.

\section{Research gap}

\textcite{el_filali_ech-chafiq_lelong_reghai_2021} demonstrate that neural networks can construct effective control variates for option-pricing integrals, including arithmetic Asian options. Their analytically integrable shallow-network construction is the basis for the NCV used here. However, their reported efficiency measure aggregates training and pricing time for a single experiment. It does not separate a reusable setup cost from the marginal cost of later valuations. This distinction matters: a method with high initial cost and low per-query cost may be inferior for one price but superior after many prices. Their robustness analysis also focuses on a dimension-reduction approach rather than on systematic frozen reuse of the shallow analytical control.

No existing study simultaneously (i) benchmarks the shallow analytical NCV against a geometric control variate under common path accounting, (ii) separates setup from marginal cost to identify a break-even point, and (iii) tests whether a network trained on one contract can be frozen and reused across a grid of related contracts.

\section{Research question and objectives}

\textbf{Research question.} Under what conditions can a trained neural control variate improve the statistical and computational efficiency of arithmetic Asian option pricing relative to standard Monte Carlo, antithetic variates and a geometric Asian control variate?

Four objectives support this question:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Compare the variance, standard error and variance-reduction ratio of the four estimators under each monitoring profile.
    \item Distinguish equal-observation performance from performance under a common simulated-path budget.
    \item Separate one-time training and pilot costs from pricing-only marginal cost, and estimate the number of valuations required to recover setup cost.
    \item Test whether a frozen reference network remains an effective control for related contracts without retraining.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope}

The analysis is restricted to discretely monitored arithmetic Asian calls under risk-neutral GBM. Two monitoring profiles are considered: $m=252$ dates (approximately business-daily) and $m=12$ dates (monthly) over a one-year horizon. These are treated as separate pricing regimes. Changing $m$ alters the payoff, the network input dimension, the number of trainable parameters and the training horizon, so the two profiles are not a controlled experiment on frequency alone.

The empirical work proceeds in two stages. Stage I trains a separate network for each contract and compares contract-specific NCV with MC, AV and GCV. Stage II freezes the network trained on a reference contract and applies it to contracts with different strikes, volatilities or maturities. The frozen control coefficient is first fixed at one and then re-estimated from an independent pilot sample. This design separates the value of the learned representation from the value of rescaling it for the target contract.

GBM is adopted as a controlled benchmark, not as a realistic model of commodity-price dynamics. A short Brent crude oil diagnostic later in the dissertation illustrates the limitations of constant volatility and Gaussian returns. Stochastic volatility, jumps and mean reversion are outside the scope of this study.
